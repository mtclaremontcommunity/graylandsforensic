COMMUNITY ALERT — GRAYLANDS & SURROUNDING SUBURBS

Will neighbourhoods around Graylands still feel safe?

The plan to more than triple forensic beds at Graylands Hospital, in Mount Claremont, raises questions for families across the surrounding suburbs. Five schools are within a kilometre. No location-specific community safety assessment has been identified in the public record — and Graylands' own record shows why that matters.

Live in Claremont, Swanbourne, Shenton Park, Floreat, Nedlands or another nearby suburb? You do not need a Mount Claremont address to have a stake in this decision. Families cross suburb boundaries for school, sport, therapy, work and everyday journeys. This page brings together the campaign's case and the evidence behind it.

A change in the campus's role and scale. Graylands has been a hospital since 1909, with a secure forensic unit added in 1993. The full long-term plan replaces general psychiatric care on the site with 136 forensic beds and 40 secure rehabilitation beds — 176 in total. Only the first stage is funded so far. That is a substantial change for an area with roughly 2,000 homes and around 4,800 students within a kilometre, including families who travel here from other suburbs. The campaign asks for the evidence that this larger campus, its security arrangements and its community-access model are suitable for this location.

What does "forensic mental health" actually mean?

Forensic mental health services combine specialist psychiatric treatment with the criminal justice system. They include people held under court orders in connection with alleged or proven serious offences, including homicide and child sexual offences. Individual legal status, treatment needs and security requirements vary. Community leave, where authorised, depends on individual assessment and approvals; it is not automatic. Supervised and unsupervised leave also existed under the previous law. The question for surrounding communities is how a much larger campus's security and community access will be managed and assessed for this location.

Where we stand

We support forensic mental health services being properly resourced in Western Australia. These patients need treatment and WA needs the capacity. Our question is whether anyone has established that this model is safe in this location — not whether the patients deserve care.

How the decision was made

The site was selected in 2021; the published comparison does not show how community safety or school proximity was assessed. Infrastructure WA subsequently found the business case, on its own, insufficient to support an investment decision. Funding followed without a published explanation of how those gaps were resolved. The Taskforce's term ended in June 2023. The government now says demand modelling included the anticipated impact of the 2023 law, but has not published the location-specific assessment the community is asking to see. The question that matters most — whether this model is safe in this location — has no published assessment behind it. We can't prove it isn't safe. The government hasn't shown it is.

The site — and the places families travel to

John XXIII College shares a boundary with the proposed expansion. Quintilian School is across the road. The Claremont Therapeutic Riding Centre — which provides riding therapy for people with disability — sits directly against it. Stage 1 alone takes forensic beds from about 45 to about 85; the full long-term plan is 136 forensic beds and 40 secure rehabilitation beds, with an estimated cost of $698.3 million. These full-plan figures extend beyond the funded first stage. No formal public consultation with residents or schools has been identified in the public record.

The warning was already on the record in 1990

In 1990, a 646-signature petition and a detailed speech by Colin Barnett to the WA Legislative Assembly raised concerns about locating a secure unit at Graylands, broken assurances to neighbours, the absence of a physical buffer, and harm following patients absconding. Barnett later served as Premier from 2008 to 2017. The campaign's safety record documents subsequent incidents in 2004, 2010, 2012, 2014 and 2015. The earlier warning and later incidents make the need for a published assessment of today's larger proposal harder to dismiss.

Eight documented concerns

Each one is drawn from a government document, a parliamentary record, or credible reporting — not from speculation. Tap a card for the short version, or jump straight to the full sourced case.

1Safety undisclosed

Community safety protocols are undisclosed

A 2014 escape went unreported to the community for a week. The campaign also documents incidents at comparable facilities. Asked in Parliament on 15 September 2026 for current protocols, construction-period arrangements and permanent upgrades, the government referred to hospital authorisation standards without describing those arrangements. Read QoN 3094 and the full answer.

Full sourced detail →
2No published security assessment

No published security assessment exists for this location

Twenty sites were compared, but the published account does not show a community-safety or school-proximity assessment. The community is asking for the full comparison and the location-specific risk assessment, including consideration of alternative settings such as the correctional precincts used by comparable facilities in NSW and Victoria.

Full sourced detail →
3Law changed

The law changed after the plan was made

The site was selected before the Criminal Law (Mental Impairment) Act 2023. In its 15 September 2026 answer, the government says demand modelling included the anticipated legislative changes, whole-of-government implementation included analysis and assessment, and community-access processes remain largely consistent with the earlier law. Those statements do not provide the location-specific assessment of the expanded campus. Read QoN 3087 and the full answer.

Full sourced detail →
4Business case gaps

The government's own adviser found the case insufficient

Infrastructure WA found the business case, on its own, had insufficient information to support an investment decision, citing six gaps. In answers on 15 September 2026, the government described the planning history and general practice on Project Definition Plans without identifying the supplementary evidence that resolved those gaps or providing the project's plan, its date or a publication link. Read QoNs 3090 and 3091.

Full sourced detail →
5Decommissioning reversed

A promised decommissioning was reversed, unexplained

For years the official plan for Graylands was decommissioning, not expansion. In Parliament, the Minister described the same Taskforce as recommending both closure and expansion in the same breath — and the public bed count has since shifted, from 53 to 40. No document explains the reversal.

Full sourced detail →
6No consultation

No formal resident or school consultation identified

The published record describes consultation with clinical professionals, public servants and lived-experience representatives. It does not establish a formal process involving residents and neighbouring schools before the site decision. The September 2026 project fact sheet still does not commit to public consultation on the siting decision. Families from other suburbs who use local schools and services also have an interest in being heard.

Full sourced detail →
7Environment, heritage & odour

Environmental and heritage concerns

Infrastructure WA flagged inadequately assessed heritage costs and environmental risks. The campaign also questions how the federal environmental referral treated staging and black cockatoo habitat. Separately, the Subiaco wastewater odour buffer remains a question for the proposed redevelopment: no project-specific public explanation has been identified showing how that constraint is resolved. Read the odour-buffer evidence.

Full sourced detail →
8Right system, right site?

No case that a single 176-bed campus is the right long-term model

WA's own psychiatrists call for a distributed, tiered forensic system, not one large campus — and say Graylands, even expanded, won't meet future need. No published document shows the concentration at this site was tested against that model, or against the CLMI Act's community-reintegration approach.

The odour buffer: a separate unresolved question

Infrastructure WA's 2023 Graylands assessment identified substantial redevelopment within the Subiaco wastewater treatment plant odour buffer as an issue requiring management. The community is asking to see the project-specific modelling, agency advice and planning basis that explain how it is being resolved.

IWA's separate assessment of the Subiaco treatment-capacity upgrade, dated December 2025 and published in June 2026, distinguishes the preferred plant upgrade from additional scope to reduce the odour buffer. The plant upgrade alone does not establish that the hospital proposal's buffer question has been settled. The report does not, by itself, prove the hospital development is unlawful or that its timetable is unworkable.
