THE PARLIAMENTARY RECORD, IN FULL

This already happened once. We know exactly how it ends.

In 1990, before the site's first secure forensic unit was even built, a 646-signature petition and a detailed speech to the WA Legislative Assembly warned government this exact plan would produce this exact set of failures. Two written assurances to this neighbourhood had already been broken. The warnings were not acted on. Everything predicted came true, repeatedly, over the following three decades. This page sets out that record in full — what was promised, what was warned, and what happened next — because in 2026, at the same site, we are being asked to trust the same silence a second time.

The same pattern, 36 years apart

Not a loose echo — the same specific moves, in the same order, at the same address.

1990

What happened then

Two written assurances to this neighbourhood were broken before a decision was made. A 646-signature petition asked for consultation before the decision, not after. A sitting MP told Parliament, in detail, which specific failures were coming. Government built the facility anyway, without the buffer or the isolation he asked for.

2026

What's happening now

No formal consultation with residents or schools has been identified anywhere in five years of planning. The government's own independent adviser found the business case inadequate; it was funded three weeks later. The specific failure mode Parliament was warned about in 1990 — transition-related absconding — is still undisclosed and unaddressed in the plan now on the table.

The difference that matters is not what happened. It's what we now know. In 1990, government could plausibly say the risks were theoretical. In 2026, they are not — they are a documented, dated, sourced 36-year record, most of it produced at this exact address. Proceeding without answering it isn't repeating a mistake. It's choosing to.

What was promised — and what happened instead

Two specific, written assurances were given to this neighbourhood before the 1989 decision to build a forensic unit at Graylands. Both were broken. This is the parliamentary record of both, side by side with what actually happened.

PROMISED — 1983

"The nature of the patient population... will not affect"

When the neighbouring Swanbourne Hospital — which held the site's forensic patients — closed in 1983, a resident named in Hansard as Mrs Jones wrote to the Health Department's Mental Health Services division, concerned that people with criminal histories would end up at Graylands instead. The Director of Mental Health Services, Mr F. Bell, replied in writing that the closure would not affect the nature of the patient population at Graylands Hospital. The two hospitals, he said, were separate entities.

WHAT HAPPENED

It did affect it. Within a few years.

By the time Colin Barnett addressed Parliament in November 1990, he stated plainly that this had not been the case since Bell's letter was written, and that some of the site's most disturbed and dangerous patients had been placed at Graylands in very low security. The written assurance did not survive contact with what government actually did next.

PROMISED — c.1983–86

"No facility for the criminally insane... would be established"

Under LandCorp's supervision, government sold the land for the neighbouring St John's Wood residential estate. Purchasers were, in Barnett's words to Parliament, of the firm opinion that no facility for the criminally insane or mentally ill offenders would be established on adjacent land. John XXIII College relocated onto the adjoining former hospital land in 1986 on a similar assurance, given in various forms by government.

WHAT HAPPENED

Government decided to build exactly that. In 1989.

Three years after John XXIII arrived, and not long after the St John's Wood estate had sold on the strength of that assurance, the State Government decided to build a dedicated maximum-security forensic unit at Graylands — the same facility both assurances had specifically ruled out. Planning decisions by government had, in Barnett's words, meant a residential area and a school were established immediately adjacent to a use government already knew it intended to change.

ASKED — 8 May 1990

"Fully discussed with and justified to the community... before"

A 646-signature petition, tabled by the then-member for Cottesloe, Bill Hassell, asked government to abandon the plan outright — or, failing that, that any future version be fully discussed with and justified to the community and all relevant authorities before a decision was made. Not after one.

WHAT HAPPENED

Neither, at the time. A narrower process, later.

Government did not abandon the plan, and did not run the kind of before-the-decision process the petition asked for. When Health Minister Peter Foss did run a public consultation in 1993, it covered only who could be admitted to the already-approved 30-bed unit — not whether to build it, or where. Full detail on that process, and how it compares to today, is in the history page.

What Parliament was warned — and what happened anyway

These are not general objections. They are specific, named mechanisms, put on the record before the facility existed, that later produced specific, documented, dated incidents.

WARNED — 29 Nov 1990

"An inherent risk... it is inevitable"

Colin Barnett told the Legislative Assembly that moving offenders progressively from high to low to no security carried a risk that could not be engineered away, and that it was inevitable — not merely possible — that mentally ill offenders would commit further offences against civil patients, residents, or children during that process. He asked for a physical buffer at the point of first transition. None was built.

WHAT HAPPENED — 2004 to 2015

The exact mechanism, on repeat.

Failures clustering specifically at transitions between security levels: a 2010 patient's repeated unauthorised absences during ward moves; a June 2014 abscond from the Frankland Centre during a security-tier transition; an October 2014 failure to return from a supervised leave arrangement — the case where the sitting Premier, Colin Barnett himself, said only that the correct procedures had been followed. WARNED — 29 Nov 1990

"Graylands has a history of security breaches"

Barnett told the House that mentally ill offenders and dangerous people had already wandered into the adjoining residential area — three people he considered dangerous had absconded that year alone, one having recently committed a serious stabbing, and in 1987 a Graylands patient in low-security care had sexually assaulted a young girl after leaving the hospital.

WHAT HAPPENED — 2004 to 2025

Absconding didn't stop. It's the most frequent risk on record.

A September 2004 escape from a unit the Attorney-General had called escape-proof; a homicide in 2018 following an absconding two days after a diagnosis later found to be wrong; a 2023 case where government's only answer, fourteen months on, was to dispute the word "escape." In the one year with a published breakdown, this hospital recorded seven times the next-highest WA facility's absconding rate. The full log →

SAID — 29 Nov 1990

"A very reasoned and responsible opposition"

Barnett pre-empted the label this kind of objection is always given: he told Parliament the opposition came from informed people, distinct from panic, and drew a direct line between community safety and patient welfare — invoking, in the same speech, "the best care and due sympathy for those patients."

36 YEARS LATER

The same characterisation is still being made — and still needs making.

This campaign supports forensic mental health services being properly resourced in Western Australia. Its concern is whether this site, this process, and this evidence base meet the standard the public is entitled to — not politics, and not the patients. That is the same distinction Barnett drew in 1990, about the same site.

The petition, and the man who warned Parliament

On 8 May 1990, the sitting member for Cottesloe, Bill Hassell, tabled a 646-signature community petition against the plan. Hassell resigned that June. The by-election that followed sent Colin Barnett to Cottesloe — Barnett went on to serve as Premier of Western Australia from 2008 to 2017. Within months of taking his seat, on 29 November 1990, he set out the full case above to the Legislative Assembly, sourced and detailed, six years before the purpose-built facility even existed.

He also put an unpublicised detail on the record that resists any simple residents-versus-patients framing: the mother of one of the state's most serious offenders had phoned him — not to attack him, but to say she hoped her own son would not be placed in a residential-area unit either, because the attention it would draw would make his rehabilitation harder.

The same man later held the office where his own warning was tested. In October 2014, a Frankland Centre patient failed to return from approved leave — the exact transition failure Barnett's 1990 speech had described. He was Premier at the time. His response was that the correct procedures had been followed: "I'm sure he'll either turn up or be found." The standard he demanded of government as a backbencher in opposition was not the standard he applied when the mechanism he had named actually failed, on his own watch.

What's changed since 1990 — and what hasn't

What's changed: the legal model. The Criminal Law (Mental Impairment) Act 2023 replaced indefinite detention with fixed limiting terms and made staged community reintegration a statutory purpose, not a discretionary exception. That is a genuine, structural change — patients now move through security tiers, toward community access, on a legislated timetable. See what the CLMI Act actually changed →

What hasn't changed: the exact mechanism Barnett warned about in 1990 is the same mechanism the CLMI Act now runs more often, not less. Every transition between security levels is a point his own speech identified as where failures cluster. A law that increases how frequently that transition happens, layered onto a site that has never published an assessment of how it manages that transition safely, is not a reason for less scrutiny of the 1990 warning. It's the reason the warning matters more now than it did then.

We are not being asked to imagine what could go wrong. We are being shown, on the public record, at this exact address, what already did — and told it won't happen again, with less published evidence for that claim than government itself provided in 1993. That is the whole case for pausing before Stage 2 proceeds.

We already know how this story ends. Let's not find out again.

Nobody in 1990 had the benefit of hindsight. Everybody in 2026 does. The petitioners of May 1990 and the MP who spoke that November were not wrong, and neither is this campaign. The difference this time is that we don't have to wait and see — we can simply ask government to show its work before an irreversible $698 million decision proceeds, using the same standard it met for a facility one-sixth this size, thirty-three years ago.
