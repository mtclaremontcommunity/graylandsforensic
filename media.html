For journalists

Covering the Graylands Campus Project

This page is a working resource, not a pitch: why this is a story now, four angles, a sourced fact sheet, and what we're not saying. Everything here is consistent with the full research paper.

Why this is a story now. Five schools sit within a kilometre of a forensic mental health campus about to nearly double in size, and enabling works started on site on 3 September 2026 — this has moved from proposal to construction. The government's own tender describes the current $197.4 million Stage 1 as the first phase of a programme once costed at $698 million. Infrastructure WA called the original business case inadequate; funding followed three weeks later, while the government's own Budget Papers still described that case as unfinished. It sits inside a wastewater-treatment odour buffer the responsible council's own planning scheme says should exclude it. No formal consultation with residents or the five nearby schools has been identified in the public record — a gap government itself closed with a real, advertised process the last time this site changed, in 1993.

Our position, quotable in full

Mount Claremont Community Network supports properly resourced forensic mental health services in Western Australia. Our position is Pause, Publish, Consult: pause site works, publish the evidence behind the decision, and hold genuine community consultation — before Stage 1, a $197.4 million first phase the government's own tender describes as part of a long-term plan once costed at $698 million, locks in the path for everything that follows.

What we are not saying

  • We are not asking for the project to be cancelled.
  • We are not saying forensic mental health patients are dangerous as a class.
  • We are not opposing mental health services at Graylands, which has provided them since 1909.
  • We are not claiming harm will occur. Our claim is narrower: nobody has published the evidence to show it won't, and a government that overrides its own independent infrastructure advisor carries the burden of showing its work.

Four story angles

Ranked for newsworthiness, not for what matters most to this campaign — those aren't the same list. Each has a suggested outlet fit, a current peg, and primary sources a reporter can check without needing to read a business case.

1. Forensic campus expansion beside five schools moves toward works — no consultation identified

Best for: television, PerthNow, The West — visual, current, a school gate and an aerial map tell it in one image.

Five schools sit within a kilometre of a forensic mental health campus about to grow from around 45 beds to 176, entirely forensic. John XXIII College shares its boundary. Enabling works were first observed on site on 3 September 2026 — this is no longer a future proposal, it's under way. Asked in its own September 2026 fact sheet what consultation had occurred, the government named clinical experts and lived-experience groups — not residents, not the schools. No formal local consultation has been identified anywhere in five years of planning. The community says it supports more forensic mental health capacity; it wants the site and security assessments released before work goes further.

2. The state's own infrastructure adviser said the case wasn't strong enough to fund — it was funded three weeks later

Best for: WAtoday, ABC politics — a clean process story with a genuine, checkable sequence.

In March 2023, Infrastructure WA — the government's statutory independent infrastructure advisor — assessed the business case and found it had "insufficient information on which to base an investment decision." Not a rejection; an evidentiary finding. The government committed $218.9M about three weeks later. Its own Budget Papers record that the money was provisioned while the Stage 1 business case was still described as "under development for future Government consideration." No document explaining what had actually changed has ever been published.

3. Government consulted neighbours before Frankland opened in 1993. Why not now?

Best for: The Post, ABC Radio Perth, a Sunday feature — archival photo available, a clean historical contrast that needs no technical explanation.

Before Graylands' first secure forensic unit opened, Health Minister Peter Foss made a public promise: no patient would be admitted until the community had been consulted on the guidelines governing admissions. Government then advertised for submissions, wrote and hand-delivered pamphlets to residents, and set a dated deadline for comment. Thirty-three years later, a change many times larger — the whole site converting to forensic use — has had no equivalent process identified. There's a 1991 photograph of the community campaign that led to that promise, and the government's own current heritage record independently confirms "much public outrage" at the time. Full account, sources and images on the history page.

4. A hospital expansion inside a sewage-treatment odour buffer

Best for: PerthNow, 6PR, The West — the curiosity hook. No background knowledge required to understand why it's strange.

The City of Nedlands' own planning scheme excludes "sensitive land uses" — hospitals included — from the mapped odour buffer around the Subiaco Wastewater Treatment Plant. Asked directly in Parliament in mid-2025 whether a risk assessment had been done, the responsible Minister's full answer was: "Yes, it has been considered as part of the project definition plan." Nothing further was volunteered — no modelling, no conclusion, no published mitigation. Full documented record, including the exact planning clause and the full Hansard exchange, on the odour buffer page.

On WA's own forensic bed shortage: we'd rather this wasn't the headline, but it's useful context if a reporter raises it. WA's psychiatrists call the forensic system "gridlocked," and in April 2026 the Supreme Court found it "impossible" to apply the new mental impairment law properly because there's no hospital capacity — a man found not criminally responsible for a killing is being held in prison indefinitely as a result ABC News, 27 Apr 2026. This campaign agrees WA needs more forensic beds. That agreement is exactly why it's asking whether concentrating all of them at one site, at this scale, is the right way to provide them — not whether they're needed at all.

If the coverage defaults to "residents vs a mental health facility"

One line of context that tends to move past it: same name, same site, new building, new model.

1909–1993
A general hospital, with no purpose-built secure unit.
1993–2026
A general hospital with a small secure wing — the Frankland Centre, 30 beds — attached, opened seven years after John XXIII College arrived on the boundary.
2026 onwards
General psychiatric care closes entirely. The site converts to a fully forensic campus, under a community-leave law that didn't exist when the site was chosen in 2021.
